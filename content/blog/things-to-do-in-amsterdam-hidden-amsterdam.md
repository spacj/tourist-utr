Top things to do in Amsterdam on foot in one afternoon start with a stroll that skips the crowds and lets the city speak to you. If you want to avoid the usual museum queues and tourist boats, a 150‑minute walk does the trick. Our favourite way to uncover the hidden side of the city is with TourHunts’ Hidden Amsterdam route – about 4.5 km, seven riddles, and a hard‑level challenge that keeps your brain as busy as your feet.

The walk weaves through centuries of history, from a quiet courtyard of devout women to the tower where Henry Hudson once set sail, giving you a tidy narrative without needing a whole day.

## Top things to do in Amsterdam on foot in one afternoon

A regular city walk shows you the sights; a puzzle walk makes you *find* them. Each clue forces you to stare at façades, alleys and bridges a little longer. Instead of breezing past a stone plaque you’ll be hunting for hints that point to a courtyard of devout women or a tower where Henry Hudson once launched his ship. The mental workout keeps the pace lively, and checking each solved clue on your phone feels oddly satisfying.

The route also strings together a story that runs from medieval religious life to the age of exploration, so you get a thematic thread without hopping between attractions all day.

## The courtyard of devout women

Tucked behind a row of classic Dutch houses lies a quiet courtyard that once served a community of devout women. Brick walls, softened by ivy, keep the space hidden from the main streets. While you wait for the next clue, listen for the faint echo of footsteps on the cobbles – it feels as if the past is still lingering.

Because the courtyard isn’t a typical tourist stop, you’ll often have it to yourself. Notice the modest altar in a corner and the small garden that once supplied herbs. It reminds you that Amsterdam’s story isn’t just about grand canals, but also about everyday lives.

## The attic church and the house with six heads

One surprising stop is a church perched in an attic space, accessed by a narrow stairwell that feels like a secret passage. From its lifted windows you can see the rooftops of the Canal Ring in a patchwork of gables. Inside, a single candle still burns, offering a quiet contrast to the busy streets below.

A short walk away stands a quirky house with six sculpted heads jutting from its façade. Local lore says the heads represent the trades that once thrived in the neighbourhood, each bearing a distinct expression. It makes a perfect photo spot; lean in and the weathered carvings are still clearly visible.

## A boat full of cats and Hudson’s launch tower

No Amsterdam walk feels complete without a nod to its waterways, and this stop features a small boat that once carried a cargo of cats – a practical way to keep ships rodent‑free. Today the boat sits near a lively market, and you can picture the tiny felines perched on deck, eyes scanning the water.

A brief stroll from the dock brings you to the tower where explorer Henry Hudson set sail for the New World. The tower now includes a modest viewing platform; from up there you can spot the convergence of several canals and, on a clear day, a sailing vessel on the horizon, echoing Hudson’s own departure centuries ago.

## Practical tips for the walk

- **Getting there**: The start point is a short walk from any tram or bike line that heads toward the city centre; the nearest stop is about five minutes away.
- **Best time**: Late spring or early autumn gives comfortable temperatures and soft light for photos. Mid‑day can be busy, so aim for a start around 10 am.
- **What to bring**: A fully charged phone (the route runs in a web browser), a water bottle and comfortable shoes. A light raincoat is wise – Dutch weather can change quickly.
- **Cost**: The first TourHunts hunt, including Hidden Amsterdam, is free. If you’ve already enjoyed another route, the next hunt costs €5.
- **Other considerations**: Keep an eye on your surroundings, especially when crossing narrow bridges. The city is flat, but some cobblestone sections can be slippery when wet.
