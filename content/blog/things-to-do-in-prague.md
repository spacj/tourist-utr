We’ve walked the cobbles of Prague many times, and we keep returning to the same handful of spots that give the city its character. In this guide we list the things to do in Prague that fit a self‑guided stroll, so you can see the highlights without racing around.

Each stop is easy to reach on foot, and we’ve added a few practical notes to keep the pace comfortable. When you’re ready for a puzzle‑style walk, TourHunts offers a free first hunt and €5 for each extra route.

## Old Town Square – a top thing to do in Prague

The historic heart of the city is Old Town Square, a wide rectangle framed by baroque churches and colourful merchant houses. The medieval Astronomical Clock puts on a short mechanical show every hour – arrive a few minutes early to claim a good bench.

A quick glance up reveals the twin spires of the Church of Our Lady before Týn; if the doors are open, a brief interior peek is worthwhile. The square also hosts a permanent market of local crafts, perfect for a leisurely browse.

**Why it works on foot**
- All the sights sit within a few minutes’ walk of each other.
- The cobbles are flat enough for most walkers, with occasional ramps for wheelchair users.
- Plenty of benches let you pause and watch the clock’s procession.

## Charles Bridge and Lesser Town

Cross the Vltava River and you’ll find Charles Bridge, 516 metres of stone lined with thirty baroque statues. Early morning is the sweet spot – the crowd thins and a light river mist adds quiet charm.

On the south side, the cobbled lanes of Lesser Town (Malá Strana) unfold. Here stands the Baroque Church of St. Nicholas, its dome striking against the sky, and the interior is richly decorated. A short detour leads to Wallenstein Garden, a formal park dotted with peacocks and a small pavilion.

**Walking tips**
- Stick to the left side of the bridge to avoid the bulk of the souvenir stalls.
- The ascent into Lesser Town is gentle; the main street, Nerudova, is paved and suitable for strollers.
- Keep an eye out for street musicians; a quick pause can add a pleasant soundtrack to your walk.

## Prague Castle and St. Vitus Cathedral

Perched on a hill above the city, Prague Castle is a sprawling complex that includes St. Vitus Cathedral, the Old Royal Palace and the Golden Lane. The grounds are mostly open and the paths well‑maintained, making a leisurely walk possible even for those who prefer a slower pace.

Inside St. Vitus, the soaring nave and stained‑glass windows offer a quiet contrast to the busy streets below. The cathedral’s treasury holds a few medieval artefacts, but the real reward is the view from the outer walls – you can see the Old Town spires and the river winding through the city.

**What to expect**
- The climb involves a series of gentle ramps; there are occasional benches for rest.
- Guided audio tours are available, but you can also read the information panels at your own speed.
- The surrounding gardens are ideal for a brief picnic if the weather allows.

## Jewish Quarter and historic synagogues

A short walk from the Old Town brings you to the Jewish Quarter (Josefov), a compact neighbourhood packed with history. The Old‑New Synagogue, dating back to the 13th century, is the oldest active synagogue in Europe and its wooden interior is strikingly simple.

Nearby, the Spanish Synagogue boasts an opulent Moorish interior, while the Jewish Museum offers a concise overview of the community’s centuries‑long presence in Prague. The quarter’s narrow lanes are lined with small courtyards that once housed families of merchants and scholars.

**Highlights for a short stroll**
- Follow the signposted route that links the main synagogues; it takes about 30 minutes.
- The area is largely flat, with even paving that’s easy on the feet.
- A modest shop near the main gate sells traditional Czech souvenirs, such as hand‑blown glass beads.

## Parks, hills and viewpoints – green spaces to unwind

Prague’s parks provide a pleasant break from the stone‑laden streets. Letná Park, on the north side of the river, offers sweeping views of the skyline, with the iconic Metronome standing where a massive statue once loomed. Wide paths make it perfect for a relaxed walk or a quick bike ride.

Further south, Petrín Hill can be reached via a historic funicular or on foot for the more energetic. At the top, the Petrín Observation Tower resembles a miniature Eiffel Tower and rewards visitors with a 360‑degree panorama. The surrounding rose garden and the mirror maze add a playful touch.

**Why include these spots**
- They are easily reachable by tram or a gentle uphill walk.
- Both locations have plenty of benches and shaded areas, ideal for a rest.
- The views are excellent for a quick photo without the crowds of the more central sites.

## Practical tips – getting around, best time to visit and what to bring

**Getting around**: Prague’s historic centre is compact enough to explore on foot, but the tram network is extensive and inexpensive. A single ticket bought from a kiosk or via the mobile app covers trams, buses and the metro for 30 minutes. Trams run frequently, and most routes stop close to the major sights mentioned above.

**Best time to visit**: Late spring (May‑June) and early autumn (September‑October) give mild temperatures and fewer tourists than the summer peak. The city’s light is especially good for photographing the rooftops and river reflections during these months.

**What to bring**: Comfortable walking shoes are essential; cobbles can be uneven. A lightweight raincoat is advisable, as showers can appear quickly.
