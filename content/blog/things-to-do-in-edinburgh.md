We’ve spent a few days wandering Edinburgh, and we’ve collected the things to do in Edinburgh that feel worth the time. From the craggy silhouette of the castle to the leafy paths of the Royal Botanic Garden, the city offers a mix of history and open air that works well on foot. Here’s a straightforward guide that points you at the postcard‑perfect spots and the corners locals drift through. All you need are comfortable shoes and a curiosity for stone streets.

## Things to do in Edinburgh: castles, streets and parks

Edinburgh Castle dominates the skyline from its perch on Castle Rock. A steep walk up the path rewards you with crown jewels and panoramic views of the city and the Firth of Forth. Just a short stroll away, the Palace of Holyroodhouse stands as the monarch’s Scottish residence, its royal apartments and historic ruins worth a peek.

If greener surroundings are more your style, head to Princes Street Gardens, which lies in the castle’s shadow. The gardens host the iconic Scott Monument and make a pleasant spot for a picnic. A few minutes north, Calton Hill offers a classic silhouette of the city’s roofs – perfect for a quick photo stop.

## The Royal Mile and its historic stops

The Royal Mile stretches like a cobbled ribbon between the castle and Holyrood Palace. Along the way, St Giles’ Cathedral rises with its striking crown steeple, while nearby John Knox House whispers tales of the Reformation. A peek into the closed‑court of Mary King’s Close gives you a glimpse of life beneath the street, and the Camera Obscura offers quirky optical tricks with a view over the Old Town.

We like to linger at the small market stalls that pop up near the foot of the Canongate, browsing local crafts without the pressure of a permanent shop. The Mile also hides historic taverns where you can sample a dram of whisky after a long walk.

## Arthur’s Seat and the surrounding hills

Arthur’s Seat, an extinct volcano, rises sharply from Holyrood Park and rewards walkers of any ability with sweeping city views. The path to the summit weaves through heather‑covered slopes and, on a clear day, reveals the harbour and the distant Pentland Hills.

For a gentler option, the nearby Salisbury Crags present dramatic cliff faces popular with climbers and photographers alike. The park also shelters a small loch and several shaded glens, making it an ideal spot for a quiet break after a city‑centre trek.

## New Town’s Georgian squares and streets

Edinburgh’s New Town, laid out in the 18th century, showcases wide avenues and elegant squares that contrast sharply with the Old Town’s narrow lanes. George Street serves as the main shopping thoroughfare, lined with polished shopfronts and a range of high‑street brands.

At the heart of the district lies Charlotte Square, a perfectly proportioned Georgian rectangle surrounded by historic townhouses. The square fronts the Scottish National Portrait Gallery, where you can admire portraits of famous Scots without spending a fortune.

A short walk north brings you to Princes Street, where the view of the castle across the Gardens is especially striking at sunset.

## The Grassmarket and its lively atmosphere

The Grassmarket sits at the foot of the castle and has a reputation for being a lively gathering place. Historically a market for livestock, it now hosts independent shops, street performers and a few traditional pubs where a local band might be playing.

From the market, a short climb up to Victoria Bridge offers a clear line of sight to the castle’s north wall. The surrounding neighbourhood is characterised by narrow closes that wind up towards the Royal Mile, perfect for a leisurely wander.

## Museums, galleries and cultural venues

Edinburgh’s cultural scene is anchored by a handful of free‑entry institutions. The National Museum of Scotland houses everything from dinosaur skeletons to a full‑size replica of a Viking ship, giving you a quick dive into natural and social history.

The Scottish National Gallery, set in a grand neoclassical building on the Mound, displays works by Rembrandt, Monet and local artists. A smaller but equally intriguing space is the Museum of Edinburgh on the Royal Mile, where you can trace the city’s evolution from a medieval settlement to a modern capital.

All of these venues sit within comfortable walking distance of each other, making it easy to fit a cultural afternoon into a single day.

## Practical tips for getting around, best time to visit and what to bring

Edinburgh’s city centre is compact enough that most attractions are reachable on foot. The public‑transport network includes frequent buses that cover the outskirts; a day ticket is a convenient option if you plan to venture beyond the centre.

Late spring or early autumn offer mild weather and fewer crowds than the peak summer months. Summer can be lively, but the rain is more likely then. Pack a waterproof jacket, sturdy shoes and a reusable water bottle – the city’s many parks and viewpoints are perfect for a quick refill.
