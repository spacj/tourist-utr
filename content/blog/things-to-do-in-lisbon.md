Things to do in Lisbon can feel overwhelming, but after a few days wandering its steep streets, hopping on tram 28 and pausing at every viewpoint, we’ve pulled together the essentials. Our aim is simple: give you a clear picture of the sights without the fluff of a typical brochure. You’ll find concrete suggestions for where to go, what to look out for, and how to stitch the bits together on foot. Let’s start at the oldest quarter and work our way outward.

## Things to do in Lisbon: Alfama and the São Jorge Castle

Alfama is the city’s oldest neighbourhood, a maze of narrow lanes that climb right up to the hilltop São Jorge Castle. The castle’s stone walls date back to the Moorish period and offer sweeping views over the red‑tiled roofs and the Tagus River. Inside the ramparts you’ll spot a small museum that explains the site’s layered history, and the open terraces are perfect for a quick photo.

A walk through Alfama is rewarding in itself. Look out for the traditional **fado houses** that line the streets; even if you don’t go inside, the sound of a mournful guitar drifting out the open doors adds atmosphere. The **Sé Cathedral**, with its Romanesque façade and Gothic interior, sits just a few minutes’ walk from the castle and makes a good first stop before you start climbing the hills.

If you’re interested in the everyday life of Lisbon’s residents, veer onto the side streets that lead to the **Mercado de Santa Clara** on market days. It’s a modest market where locals sell fresh produce, cheese and the occasional antique. The whole area is best explored on foot, with plenty of spots to pause and watch the world go by.

## Baixa, Rossio and the Elevador de Santa Justa

The Baixa district was rebuilt after the 1755 earthquake, giving it a grid of wide avenues and grand plazas. **Praça do Comércio** fronts the river with a monumental arch and a statue of King José I; the square feels grand yet open enough for a leisurely stroll.

From there, head up **Rua Augusta** to the **Arco da Rua Augusta**, where you can climb a short flight of stairs for a modest panorama of the downtown rooftops. A short walk brings you to **Rossio Square**, famous for its wave‑patterned paving and the **National Theatre** façade. The square is a natural meeting point for tram 28, which you can hop on for a quick ride to other parts of the city.

One of Baixa’s quirkiest attractions is the **Elevador de Santa Justa**, a wrought‑iron lift that connects the lower streets to the **Carmo** square above. The lift itself is an architectural curiosity, and the viewing platform at the top offers a different angle on the city’s tiled roofs. The ride is cheap, and the experience is worth the short wait during peak hours.

While you’re in the area, keep an eye out for the **Café A Brasileira** on **Rua Garrett**. It’s a historic coffee house where you can sip a strong espresso while people‑watch from the outdoor terrace.

## Belém: monuments and riverside walks

Cross the river on the 25 April Bridge and you’ll find the Belém district, a showcase of Portugal’s Age of Discovery. The **Jerónimos Monastery** dominates the skyline with its intricate Manueline stonework; even from the outside the cloisters hint at the craftsmanship inside.

Just a stone’s throw away stands the **Belém Tower**, a fortified lighthouse that once guarded the harbour. Its white façade reflects the river’s light, making it a favourite spot for photographers. Nearby, the **Monument to the Discoveries** stretches along the waterfront, celebrating explorers like Vasco da Gama.

All these sites are linked by a pleasant promenade that runs alongside the Tagus. The path is flat and perfect for a relaxed walk, with benches and occasional street performers. If you feel like a snack, look for a family‑run bakery that sells the famous **pastéis de nata** – a custard tart that’s best enjoyed warm.

Belém also houses the **Museu Nacional dos Coches**, home to an impressive collection of historic carriages. The display offers a tangible glimpse into royal transport from centuries past.

## Chiado and Bairro Alto: cafés, streets and viewpoints

Chiado sits between the historic Baixa and the vibrant Bairro Alto, offering a blend of elegant shops, literary cafés and cultural venues. The **Café A Brasileira** (different from the one in Baixa) has been a meeting point for writers since the early 20th century; its mirrored walls and vintage décor make it a nice spot for a quick break.

Wander down **Rua Garrett** and you’ll encounter the **São Carlos National Theatre**, an opulent building whose interior is worth a peek if you catch a rehearsal. The surrounding streets are lined with bookshops and small galleries, perfect for a slow walk.

When the sun starts to set, head up to **Miradouro de São Pedro de Alcântara** in Bairro Alto. The viewpoint offers a sweeping panorama that includes the castle, the river and the red‑tiled roofs stretching to the horizon. It’s a popular spot for locals to gather, so you’ll find a few street musicians adding ambience.

Bairro Alto itself is a maze of narrow lanes filled with bars and small eateries. Even if you’re not looking for a night out, the area’s lively street life gives you a sense of contemporary Lisbon culture. Stick to the main squares if you prefer a quieter stroll.

## Parques and Gardens: Eduardo VII, Jardim da Estrela and beyond

Lisbon’s green spaces provide a welcome contrast to its steep streets. **Parque Eduardo VII** sits at the northern edge of the city centre, offering wide avenues, a central lake and a viewpoint that looks out over the river. The park’s **Greenhouse (Estufa Fria)** houses exotic plants and is a pleasant detour for nature lovers.

Further west, the **Jardim da Estrela** surrounds the **Basilica of Estrela**, a striking baroque church with twin bell towers. The garden’s pathways wind through manicured lawns, rose beds and a small pond, making it an ideal place for a leisurely pause.

If you have extra time, consider a walk through the **Gulbenkian Garden**, a hidden oasis that combines formal French designs with Japanese elements. The garden is part of a larger cultural complex that also includes the **Gulbenkian Museum**, which holds an impressive collection ranging from ancient artefacts to modern art. The garden itself is free to explore.

These parks are especially pleasant in the early morning or late afternoon, when the light softens and the city’s hum eases.

## Practical tips: getting around, best time to visit and what to bring

**Getting around** – Lisbon’s historic centre is compact enough for most attractions to be reached on foot. For longer distances, the **metro** and **tram 28** are reliable and cheap. Purchase a reusable travel card; it works on buses, trams and the metro, and you can top it up as needed.

**Best time to visit** – Late spring (May to June) and early autumn (September to October) offer mild temperatures and fewer crowds than the peak summer months. The city’s hills are pleasant for walking, and the light is ideal for photography.

**What to bring** – Comfortable walking shoes are essential; cobblestones can be uneven. A light rain jacket is wise, as showers can appear unexpectedly. If you plan to use the tram, a small day‑pack will keep your belongings secure.
