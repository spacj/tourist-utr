We love a good walk through a city that wears its history on its walls, and Florence gives us plenty of that. In this guide we list the things to do in Florence that work best on foot, from grand squares to quiet lanes, so you can plan a satisfying trip without a packed itinerary.

Our aim is to keep the advice practical and the tone friendly – think of us as a well‑travelled friend pointing out where to linger, where to snap a photo, and which cobbles are worth the extra step.

## The Heart of the City: Piazza del Duomo

First, stand in the shadow of the Cathedral of Santa Maria del Fiore and let its massive dome dominate your view – Brunelleschi’s masterpiece still defines Florence’s skyline. A short stroll brings you to the Baptistery, its bronze doors a quiet counterpoint to the cathedral’s grandeur. If you’re feeling energetic, climb Giotto’s Campanile; the 360° panorama of red‑tile roofs is worth the effort.

**Key points to check off:**
- The Cathedral interior, with its frescoed ceiling.
- The Baptistery’s “Gates of Paradise”.
- The Campanile for a panoramic walk.
- The Museum of the Opera del Duomo for deeper context.

After ticking the boxes, let the surrounding lanes draw you in – the stone paving is uneven, so sturdy shoes are advisable.

## Renaissance Art at the Uffizi Gallery

Inside the Uffizi, you’ll find the world’s most concentrated collection of early Italian Renaissance paintings. Seeing Botticelli’s *Birth of Venus* or Leonardo’s *Annunciation* in person adds texture you simply can’t capture on a screen. Arriving early helps you dodge the longest queues, and the gallery’s logical layout makes a self‑guided route easy to follow.

**Highlights to look for:**
- Botticelli’s *The Birth of Venus* and *Primavera*.
- Leonardo da Vinci’s *Annunciation*.
- Michelangelo’s *Doni Tondo*.
- Caravaggio’s dramatic *Medusa*.

When you need a break, the museum’s café offers a decent coffee, and the nearby corridors provide quiet corners for a quick sketch.

## Strolling the Arno: Ponte Vecchio and Oltrarno

Cross the Arno on the Ponte Vecchio and you’ll feel the weight of centuries beneath your feet. The narrow shops, now mostly jewellers, have been there since medieval times, and from the centre you can spot the Vasari Corridor snaking above the river.

On the opposite bank, the Oltrarno neighbourhood invites you to wander past artisan workshops, tucked‑away squares and the impressive Palazzo Pitti. Behind the palace, the Boboli Gardens stretch up a gentle hill, offering shaded paths and occasional city vistas.

Both sides of the river exude a relaxed vibe – perfect for a gelato while you watch the water flow.

## Historic Markets and Food Streets

Florence’s markets are a feast for the senses. The Mercato Centrale, housed in a grand iron structure, overflows with fresh produce, cheeses and cured meats. Just a short walk away, the San Lorenzo outdoor market feels chaotic but rewarding, with vendors shouting the day’s best deals.

The surrounding streets – especially Via de’ Neri and Via della Vigna – are peppered with family‑run bakeries, tiny osterias and street stalls selling lampredotto or truffle sandwiches. Allocate an hour or two to simply wander, sample a few bites, and watch locals go about their day.

## Neighbourhood Walks: San Lorenzo and Santa Croce

Start in the San Lorenzo quarter, anchored by the Basilica of San Lorenzo. Its stark interior, funded by the Medici, contrasts sharply with the ornate façade, and the nearby Medici Chapels showcase striking marble tombs.

A short stroll leads you to the Santa Croce basilica, where Michelangelo, Galileo and Machiavelli rest. The square in front buzzes with life, while the surrounding lanes host artisan workshops and modest cafés.

Both neighbourhoods reward a leisurely pace; narrow streets often open onto quiet squares where you can pause for a cheap espresso and watch the world go by.

## Practical Tips for Getting Around Florence

Florence’s historic centre is compact enough that most sights sit within a ten‑minute walk of each other. The city’s wide avenues are mostly pedestrianised, though you’ll still encounter occasional traffic on the main arteries.

**Best time to visit**: late spring or early autumn brings mild weather and fewer crowds, making the walks more pleasant. Summer can be hot, so carry water and a hat.

**What to bring**: comfortable shoes, a reusable water bottle, a small map or a charged phone for navigation. A light raincoat is useful in the occasional April shower.
